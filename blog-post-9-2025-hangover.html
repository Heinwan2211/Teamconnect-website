The 2025 Hangover — Why Your Team Came Back Already Tired

✎ Team Connect Insights 📅 August 2026 ⏱ 6 min read

2025 was a hard year. Most South African corporates knew that at the time. What fewer expected is that 2026 would arrive without a proper reset — and that eight months in, the fatigue hasn't eased. If anything, it's compounded. Here's what we're hearing from clients, and why the recovery most leaders assumed would happen naturally hasn't.

At the start of 2026, most managers we speak with expected a rebound. The 2024-2025 stretch had been brutal — load-shedding at its worst, currency volatility, restructures across almost every sector, and the accumulated weight of "post-pandemic" workloads that never actually normalised. But teams had made it through. December brought a break, then January, and by February most leaders assumed the recovery had begun.

It hasn't.

Nine months into the year, we're hearing the same pattern from clients across financial services, retail, technology, and the public sector: the December break wasn't a reset. It was a pause. Teams came back in January looking rested for about three weeks, and by mid-February the underlying fatigue had resurfaced. Now, halfway through Q3, the pattern is unmistakable — and the leaders we're working with are quietly worried.

What's actually different this time

Team fatigue isn't new. What's different in 2026 is the compounding. Most of the managers we work with are describing something that looks less like a bad quarter and more like a slow accumulation with no clear turning point.

Three patterns come up repeatedly in the conversations we're having:

People are working, but they're not fully present. Attendance is fine. Meetings are attended. Deliverables ship. But the discretionary effort — the thing that separates a functional team from a great one — has thinned out. Senior leaders describe it as "the team is doing the job, but I can't feel them in the room anymore." Nobody's quitting loudly. Nobody's obviously disengaged. But something has quietly stepped back.

The recovery signals aren't landing. Where an away day or a long weekend in the past would produce a visible lift in energy, those interventions now produce a much shorter bounce. A wellness Friday that used to buy a team two months of momentum now buys two weeks. Managers who've done everything by the book are asking themselves whether the tools they've relied on for years still work.

Quiet exits are up. Not the loud, high-drama resignations of 2022. These are the mid-performers who've been steady for years and are suddenly having conversations with recruiters. When we speak to HR directors, the phrase we hear most often is "we didn't see it coming" — which is precisely the problem. Twelve to eighteen months of low-grade attrition risk is finally catching up with organisations that thought they'd weathered the storm.

Why the December break didn't fix it

The instinctive response, when someone is tired, is that they need rest. And that's true — but only up to a point. A one-week break, or even three, doesn't repair eighteen months of accumulated load. It provides temporary relief. Actual recovery requires something structurally different: a change in the pattern of work, not just a pause from it.

South African corporates are largely still running the same operating rhythm they ran through 2024 and 2025. The demands haven't shifted. The team compositions haven't shifted. In most cases, the leadership hasn't shifted either. So the recovery that a December break was supposed to enable never had the structural support to actually take hold. People came back to the same job, the same meetings, and the same weight — just marginally more rested.

By March, they were where they had been in November.

What we're seeing that actually helps

The organisations we're working with that are handling this well have done something specific: they've stopped treating team wellbeing as an event and started treating it as a rhythm.

What that looks like in practice varies, but the pattern is consistent. Rather than one big wellness intervention per year (which never worked as well as its budget suggested), these organisations are building smaller, more frequent moments of recovery into the calendar. A one-hour team check-in every six weeks with a proper facilitator. A quarterly reset session that combines development work with genuine reflection on how the team is holding up. Regular access to clinical wellbeing support — not just when someone reaches a crisis, but preventatively.

The shift is from "we'll do something big when things get bad" to "we'll do small things consistently so things don't get bad in the first place." It's cheaper, more sustainable, and — crucially — it works.

They need an ongoing conversation about their team's capacity, and they need a partner who can help them design one.

What you can do in the next 30 days

If the pattern above sounds familiar, three things are worth doing before the year-end rush amplifies whatever's already there.

Take an honest read on your team. Not through a survey — those tend to surface what people think they should say. Through a real conversation with each of your direct reports. Ten minutes each. Ask them: "What's been harder this year than it should have been, and what's carrying over from 2025 that hasn't fully gone away?" Then be quiet and listen. The pattern will emerge fast.

Don't wait for December to plan recovery. Whatever you build for year-end, layer in something before then. A quarterly team development session in October. A shortened week in September. A properly-facilitated team reset that combines reflection with real recovery. December is too late — by the time the break arrives, the damage is done.

Treat wellbeing as infrastructure, not an event. The organisations coming out of 2026 in better shape aren't the ones with the flashiest annual offsite. They're the ones with a rhythm of small, consistent interventions that keep the team's tank from emptying in the first place.

Before Q4 arrives

The year isn't over, and the pattern isn't fixed. But the organisations that will start 2027 in a genuinely different place are the ones acting now — not the ones hoping December will do the work for them again.
