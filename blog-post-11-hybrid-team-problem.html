The Hybrid Team Problem — Why “Sometimes in the Office” Isn’t Working

✎ Team Connect Insights 📅 October 2026 ⏱ 7 min read

The problem with most South African hybrid policies isn’t that they’re hybrid. It’s that they’re vague. “Two or three days in the office” sounds like a policy; in practice it’s an absence of one. And the cost of that vagueness is showing up in exactly the places managers assumed hybrid would help.

Three years in, most of the corporates we work with are still running “hybrid” in the loosest sense of the word. The policy says a number of days. It doesn’t say which days. It doesn’t say who needs to be there at the same time. It leaves the pattern to the individual, and the individual — reasonably — picks whatever suits their week.

The result is predictable, and it’s not good. Teams never have a critical mass in the office at the same time. People drive in to do calls they could have done from home. The office becomes a place you go to attend Zoom meetings in a slightly nicer chair.

What most “hybrid” policies actually are

Strip away the language, and most South African hybrid policies have exactly one requirement: a quota. Two days. Three days. Sixty percent. “Most of the week.” The quota is what gets measured, so the quota is what people hit — but a quota is not a pattern.

When everyone chooses their own days, the aggregate behaviour is random. On Tuesday, half the team is in. On Wednesday, a different half. By Thursday, the three people who actually needed to collaborate are all at home, each wondering why nobody else came in. Nobody is breaking the rules. The rules just don’t say what they need to say.

And once this becomes the norm, the compounding effects set in quickly. Nobody plans important conversations for the office, because they can’t predict who will be there. So those conversations default to Teams or Zoom, which means the office empties further, which means even fewer reasons to come in. It’s a quiet downward spiral masked by the fact that the headline number — days in office per week — stays roughly the same.

Why ambiguity is more expensive than either extreme

Fully remote teams work because they have to. The absence of in-person contact forces explicit written decisions, async communication norms, and a culture of writing things down. It’s not natural, but it’s consistent, and good remote teams build the muscles to make it work.

Fully in-person teams work because in-person collaboration is cheap. Hallway conversations, impromptu whiteboard sessions, the five-minute desk-side chat that resolves what would have been a week-long email thread — all of this is available constantly, without being scheduled.

Ambiguous hybrid teams get the worst of both. The written norms don’t develop, because “we’ll just talk about it tomorrow” feels possible — but tomorrow the right people aren’t there. The hallway conversations don’t happen, because there’s no hallway with the right people in it. What remains is a coordination overhead that nobody budgets for and nobody fixes, because nobody can point at a single thing that’s broken.

“The office becomes a place you go to attend Zoom meetings in a slightly nicer chair.”

What this is quietly doing to your team

The costs of ambiguous hybrid don’t show up as a line on anyone’s dashboard. They show up as a slow erosion of things that are hard to measure but easy to feel.

Trust decays. People trust the colleagues they interact with regularly. When you see someone twice a month in a scheduled meeting, you have a working relationship; you don’t have the trust that carries a team through a difficult quarter. Hybrid-by-quota slowly starves that trust without ever pointing to a moment it broke.

New joiners struggle. The person who joined six weeks ago has no consistent presence around them to learn from. They’re on calls with people they’ve never shared a coffee with. The implicit knowledge of “how we actually do things here” takes months longer to absorb, and some of it never does.

Culture thins out. Culture isn’t built in quarterly offsites. It’s built in the small, repeated moments of being together — and when those moments are random and unreliable, culture becomes thinner, less specific, easier to lose.

Managers over-schedule meetings to compensate. When informal contact disappears, the instinct is to create formal contact. The calendar fills up with status check-ins, syncs, and alignment calls that wouldn’t have existed if people were simply in the same room sometimes. The cure becomes part of the disease.

What actually works

The teams getting hybrid right have done one specific thing: they’ve replaced the quota with a pattern. Not necessarily more days in the office — often fewer. Just a shape that everyone knows.

The two patterns we see working are at opposite ends of the spectrum, which is the point. The middle is where things fall apart.

The first is team-anchored days. “We’re all in on Tuesday and Thursday.” Not individually chosen. Not optional. Those are the days the team is together, and everything that benefits from being together — strategy conversations, new-joiner integration, hard feedback, creative work — is deliberately scheduled for them. The other three days are protected for focused individual work, wherever people choose to do it. The team gets the compound interest of real contact without the overhead of being in the office to do solo work.

The second is genuinely remote-first with intentional gather points. The team is remote by default, with written communication norms, async-first meetings, and the proper tooling for distributed work. Once a quarter — or more often if the work needs it — the whole team gathers for two or three days in person, with a real agenda: the kinds of conversations that are expensive to have asynchronously. This is more disciplined than it sounds, but it works, especially for teams spread across Johannesburg, Cape Town, and Durban where consistent in-office days aren’t realistic anyway.

Either shape works. The middle — “two or three days, pick your own” — is the one that doesn’t.

What to do this quarter

If you’re a manager or team lead, you don’t need a company-wide policy change to fix this. You need to pick a pattern for your own team and defend it.

Audit what you’ve actually got. Not what the policy says — what people are doing. For the past four weeks, how often has your team been in the office on the same day? If the answer is “rarely,” you don’t have a hybrid team. You have an individually-remote team that occasionally overlaps.

Pick the shape. Team-anchored days or genuinely remote-first with planned gather points. Not both, not something in between. Choose by what your team’s work actually needs, not by what feels diplomatic.

Defend it against drift. This is the part most managers skip. The pattern will erode within a month if you don’t actively protect it — people will skip the anchored day for a client meeting, a dentist appointment, a family thing. Those requests are all reasonable individually. Honour them, but hold the line that the anchored day is the default, not a suggestion.

Pick a pattern

Hybrid work isn’t the problem. Ambiguous hybrid is. The managers whose teams are thriving in this model haven’t found some clever third way — they’ve just decided on a shape and stuck to it. The sooner you pick one for your team, the sooner the quiet costs stop accumulating.
